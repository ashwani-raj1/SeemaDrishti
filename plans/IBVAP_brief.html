Problem Statement 26187 · Ministry of Home Affairs

Intelligent Border Video Analytics Platform

Software that turns cameras India already owns into a system that actually watches.

India's border forces have thousands of ordinary CCTV cameras along the fence, at border posts and at check posts. Those cameras record. They don't notice. Noticing is left to two or three people staring at screens through the night. This document explains a software layer that does the noticing — and, more importantly, explains why most of the obvious ways to build it have already failed in the field.

Orientation

Start here: what exists today, and what's missing

Skip nothing in this section — everything later depends on it.

A Border Out Post (BOP) is a small manned post along the international border. India has thousands of them, plus check posts on roads and cameras along border roads. Many already have ordinary IP cameras — the same kind used in an office building, sending video over a network cable using a standard called RTSP.

Those cameras do two things: they show live video on a monitor, and they save it to a hard disk. That's it. If a person climbs the fence at 2 a.m., the camera captures it perfectly and tells nobody. A human has to be looking at that exact tile on that exact screen at that exact moment.

The obvious fix is to buy smarter cameras — ones with face recognition or number-plate reading built in. India has tried this. It's expensive, the hardware is proprietary, spare parts are hard to get in remote areas, and every installation drags along land acquisition and procurement delays that have historically run into years.

Why this problem statement exists at all

An audit found 66% of border land-acquisition cases delayed — the worst by nine years. Roughly 24,000 acres in Punjab border villages have been disputed since 1988. Every hardware-based solution inherits that entire delay chain.

Software running on cameras that are already installed skips all of it. That is the real argument for this project — not that AI is impressive.

So the goal is a pure-software layer: read the video these cameras already produce, understand what's in it, and tell someone when it matters. No new cameras, no proprietary boxes.

The trap almost everyone falls into

Here's the thing that makes this project interesting rather than routine. India already has a large electronic border surveillance programme. Sensors, thermal cameras, radar, laser fences. And the single biggest documented reason those pilots struggled was not that they failed to detect people.

It's that they detected everything. Cattle. Wind in the grass. Rain. Dogs. A stray nilgai. An operator who gets 200 false alarms a night stops trusting the system by the third night — and after that, the real alarm looks exactly like the other 199.

The claim this project makes

Not "we can detect intruders." That is already solved, several times over, by systems in the field right now.

The claim is "we can detect intruders without crying wolf" — and everything in this design follows from that one sentence.

The organising rule

Three layers, built in order

This project has a discipline problem baked into it. The clever, memorable, differentiating ideas all sit on top of a fairly ordinary base. If you build the clever parts first, you end up with something that looks impressive and doesn't answer the actual requirement.

So the work is split into three layers, and the rule is: build downward from Layer 0, never sideways.

Layer 1 scope

The five problems we chose to solve

Research turned up fourteen documented, sourced problems that Indian border forces face with surveillance technology. Trying to solve all fourteen would produce fourteen shallow features. Five were chosen — the five where software on existing cameras is genuinely the right tool.

Each one below is a real, evidenced problem. The numbering (P1, P2…) comes from the research catalogue and is used throughout this document.

P1 · Detection qualitythe big one
False alarms, not missed detections, are what killed previous systems

The #1 documented failure of India's existing border surveillance pilots was false alerts from wildlife, weather and vegetation.

What it means for us: the entire value of this platform rests on filtering, not on detecting. This reframes the product.

P2 · Night & fogthe threat window
The border goes blind during exactly the months infiltrators use

From mid-December to February, dense fog drops visibility to near zero at dawn and dusk. Forces confirm infiltrators deliberately exploit it, and that existing equipment capability "declines drastically" in that window.

What it means for us: low visibility isn't an edge case to handle later. It's the main event.

P3 · Operator loada UI problem
There is nobody available to watch the screens

BSF is short 12,808 personnel, gets pulled onto election and other non-border duties, and a post's control room is manned round the clock by two or three people.

What it means for us: this is not a detection problem. It's an interface problem. Design for one pair of eyes on an eight-hour shift.

P6 · No power, no signalarchitecture
Many posts have no electricity and effectively no connectivity

Several border posts — especially in the hills — lack electricity entirely. Floating river posts have "very poor cellular connectivity." Over 4,210 km of access roads are still needed.

What it means for us: anything cloud-dependent fails the requirement on its own terms. Everything must run locally.

P9 · Dronespartly out of reach
Small drones are the dominant current threat on the Punjab border

Drones recovered in Punjab: 107 in 2023 → 245 in 2024 → 272+ in 2025. Mostly sub-1 kg DJI Mavics carrying 500–600 g of contraband.

What it means for us: a ground-facing CCTV camera cannot see one of these. We address two reachable angles instead — see Layer 1.

On the nine problems left out

The other nine documented problems — including that interception, not detection, is the real operational gap, and that equipment breaks with no repair structure — are real and were deliberately set aside. Each has a one-line holding answer prepared, because a knowledgeable reviewer may well ask about them.

Product thesis

One platform, many forces

This is stated as a design goal, not as a problem being fixed. The problem statement asks the solution to "support integration with existing command and control systems" and to be deployable across "remote border locations and strategic installations."

That last phrase does a lot of work. Strategic installations is the brief quietly saying "not only the border fence." Naval jetties. Mountain passes. Restricted-area perimeters.

So the goal is one platform, one data model, one program — usable by any force, without being customised for any of them. Not a BSF tool and a separate Navy tool. The same primitives, configured differently.

Why this matters more than it sounds

It converts a claim into something you can watch happen: the running program switches from a border-fence setup to a naval-waterline setup, live, in about thirty seconds. Same code, same database, same screen — only the config changed.

And unlike most impressive demos, this one needs no AI computation at all, so it works on any laptop.

Layer 0

What the problem statement demands

Eight capabilities, two constraints, one integration requirement. Non-negotiable, and complete before anything else gets attention.

The eight capabilities

01
Find people and follow them

Spot a person in the video, draw a box around them, and keep the same identity on them frame after frame as they move. "Tracking" means the system knows frame 200's person is the same one from frame 100 — not a new person each time.

Note Table stakes. Every competing team will have this. Zero differentiation on its own.

02
Find vehicles and say what kind

Car, truck, two-wheeler, tractor — and, where it matters, boat. The word classification in the brief is doing real work: it asks us to tell categories apart, which is the hook the false-alarm fix hangs on.

03
Virtual fence

Draw a line or shape on the camera's view and get told when someone crosses it. No physical sensor — just geometry over the video.

Note The naive version — "line crossed: yes/no" — is destroyed by P1 and floods with lawful farmer traffic. Layer 1 hardens it.

04
Live alerts and a record of everything

Raise an alarm as it happens, and keep a searchable log afterwards.

Note Where P3 bites hardest. A system that emits 4,000 events a shift to a three-person room has satisfied the words of the brief and failed its intent.

05
Face detection

Notice that a face is present. Note carefully: the brief says detection, not identification. Finding a face and knowing whose face it is are completely different problems, with completely different consequences.

06
Number plate reading (ANPR)

Read a vehicle's registration plate from video.

Note Honestly unreliable on an open border road at night — glare, angle, speed, plate variation. Layer 2 scopes it to where it genuinely works: slow, close, head-on, at a controlled crossing point.

07
Suspicious activity detection

The vaguest line in the brief. Deliberately not built as a machine-learning "suspicion" classifier — see Layer 1 for what replaces it and why.

08
Night-time movement detection

Detect movement in the dark. Looks like a minor line item. Because of P2, it is the single most consequential capability on this list.

The two constraints

09
Software only — no special hardware

It must work on standard IP cameras already installed. No proprietary smart cameras, no dedicated face-recognition or plate-reading boxes. This constraint is the project.

10
Read standard camera streams

Speak RTSP and ONVIF — the ordinary, universal languages IP cameras use. If a camera works with generic recording software, it must work with this.

And the integration requirement

11
Plug into existing command and control systems

Forces already have control-room software. This must feed into it rather than replace it.

Where this leads Built honestly, it means a documented event format, an outward feed — and crucially an inward one too, so other equipment can send events to us. That inbound direction turns out to be how the drone problem gets solved.

Layer 1

Making it survive the real world

Nothing in this layer is a new capability. Every item exists to make a Layer 0 capability work somewhere that has fog, no electricity, and three tired people.

The #numbers continue from Layer 0 and are used consistently across all project documents.

Detection quality

P1 · false alarms
12
Tell animals from people — the highest-value item in this entire document

Add cattle, dogs, nilgai and wild boar as things the system recognises on purpose, plus a check that rejects the shimmer of moving vegetation. An animal crossing the virtual fence is written to the log and never alerted on.

On the eastern border this doubles as real detection: a herd moving toward the river at 2 a.m. is a cattle-smuggling alert. One cow is not.

Why it fits It converts the pitch from "we detect intruders" — already solved — to "we detect intruders without crying wolf," which the failure record says is genuinely unsolved.

Risk Standard pre-trained models are weak on nilgai and wild boar, so this needs a small India-specific training set. And failing the other way — suppressing a real human — is the worst outcome in the whole system. That's exactly why #15 exists.

13
Wait a moment before shouting

Require a crossing to persist for a sustained period before a critical alarm fires, instead of reacting to a single frame's flicker.

Risk This trades speed for accuracy, and it's configurable per zone — two seconds of delay is defensible, fifteen is not. The trade is stated openly rather than hidden.

14
Learn what "normal" looks like at each camera

Each camera learns its own usual activity level by hour of day and day of week, so sensitivity can rise during the foggy months and relax during monsoon vegetation movement. "Normal" becomes local to each post rather than one global setting.

Why it fits It's the only way to reduce false alarms across thousands of very different cameras without hand-tuning each one. The brief asks for scalable.

Hard restriction Aggregate counts per camera only — never per-person behaviour profiles. That line is not crossed.

15
A "second look" queue

Uncertain and suppressed detections go into a short review list rather than vanishing. What the operator decides becomes training data.

Why it fits It's the safety net that makes #12's suppression defensible, and it slowly builds the India-specific dataset that doesn't currently exist.

Risk It adds operator work — the exact thing we're reducing. Hard cap on queue length; idle-time work, never an obligation.

Night and fog

P2 · the threat window
16
Brighten the picture before looking at it

A standard contrast-enhancement technique applied to dark frames before detection runs. Cheap, and only switched on when the picture is actually dark — running it in daylight wastes effort for nothing.

17
Say out loud when you've gone blind

The system continuously measures how much it can actually see. When visibility collapses, the screen says so — "Camera 14: DEGRADED — confidence ceiling 0.55" — instead of quietly missing people.

It also steps down through defined stages rather than failing silently: full analysis → motion only → recording only. Every step is announced.

Why it fits The documented failure of existing systems is silent capability loss during exactly the window infiltrators exploit. Knowing where you are blind is situational awareness, which is what the brief asks us to improve.

The honest part Contrast enhancement recovers very little in genuinely zero-visibility fog. That's physics, not tuning. We do not claim to solve fog. We claim to detect and declare our own blindness so the operator knows to send a foot patrol. Smaller claim, far more credible.

The operator's screen

P3 · UI/UX only
18
Show incidents, not cameras — the design inversion

Every competing team will build a grid of sixteen camera tiles. A three-person control room cannot watch sixteen tiles. So the default screen is a ranked list of open incidents, each expandable to its video. The camera grid becomes a secondary tab.

Acknowledge, escalate and dismiss are keyboard keys, so an operator never hunts for a small target with a mouse at 3 a.m.

19
Group related detections into one incident

One person crossing one fence generates dozens of raw detections. Those get bundled into a single incident. The operator sees roughly eight incidents a shift, not four thousand events.

Risk Over-aggressive grouping could merge two genuinely separate intrusions. Grouping is capped by individual track, and the underlying events always remain inspectable.

20
Three severity levels, not an on/off alarm

Info, Warning, Critical — so attention can be rationed. Most teams build a binary alarm.

21
Always show why the alarm fired

Every alert displays the box around the subject, the path they walked, the zone they crossed, the named rule that triggered, and how confident the system was.

Why it fits Technical expertise isn't evenly available across the force. An alert nobody can explain is an alert that gets ignored. This is also the structural answer to "black-box AI at the border."

22
A live status board of posts and cameras

What's up, what's degraded, what's blind right now.

23
Shift handover summary

An automatic end-of-shift digest: what fired, what was acknowledged, what's still open, which cameras degraded. Tiny feature; disproportionate credibility with anyone who knows what a round-the-clock three-person room actually feels like.

Runs without the internet

P6 · cloud-independent
24
Zero cloud dependency — a hard architectural commitment, not a "mode"

No cloud service anywhere in the working path. No external AI service, no internet-based login, no hosted database. A post with no connectivity runs the complete feature set locally — detection, alerts, dashboard, storage, permissions.

Why it fits The brief says "remote border locations." P6 says many posts have neither mains power nor usable mobile signal. A cloud-dependent design fails the brief on its own terms — and it's the only defensible posture for a border security system regardless.

25
Think locally, send summaries

The analysis happens at the post. What travels upward is a short text record plus a thumbnail — never a live video stream. Full clips stay on local disk and are fetched only if someone asks.

26
Queue and forward

If the link to headquarters drops, events pile up locally and sync when it returns. Nothing is lost, nothing duplicated.

27
Every post declares what it's running

Each node periodically reports its software version, its configuration and its active zones — so headquarters can answer "what is actually deployed out there?" rather than assuming. Any drift becomes visible instead of invisible.

Drones

P9 · two reachable angles

The honest starting position: a ground-facing CCTV camera cannot see a sub-1 kg drone. It's a handful of pixels against a cluttered sky, and the cameras point at the ground anyway. Claiming otherwise in front of people who deal with this weekly would be a credibility disaster. Two things we can do:

28
Spot the pickup, not the aircraft

A drone drops a packet; a person on this side has to go and collect it. That produces a recognisable ground pattern: travel to an unusual spot → pause briefly → return along the same path. That is visible to a ground camera, and it's a plain rule — explainable, adjustable, no training data needed.

29
Accept alerts from equipment that can see drones — the clever one

Forces already deploy radio-frequency drone detectors and radar. We don't compete with those sensors; we absorb them. Their alert arrives into our system as an ordinary event, in the same format as a camera detection, and gets stitched to the ground video into a single incident:

RF contact 02:14 grid 4B → person detected grid 4B 02:19 → retrieval pattern confirmed 02:21

Why it fits This is precisely what the brief's "integration with existing command and control systems" line is for. It also demonstrates the value of the shared data model better than any single feature could — and it lets us speak credibly about the force's biggest current worry without claiming a capability we don't have.

Risk We have no real RF sensor to connect to, so the demonstration uses a clearly labelled simulated sensor. Never implied to be real.

30
Map the drop hotspots

Over time, aggregate past events into a map of where consignments tend to land. Zone-level patterns only — never individual profiles.

Layer 2

The platform layer

This is the part that makes the project distinctive. It's justified on capability — it makes the system genuinely more useful — not on ethics or presentation.

A shared, typed data model

the good idea worth borrowing
31
Connected objects instead of flat log lines

Most systems write log entries: "person detected, Camera 7, 02:14." Useful once, useless afterwards. Instead, everything becomes a linked object with a defined type — organisation, site, camera, zone, tracked thing, event, incident, alert, action.

The difference in practice: "this tracked figure crossed this zone on this camera, was seen again on Camera 9 four minutes later, is linked to this vehicle, produced this incident, which this operator escalated at 02:19 for this stated reason." That is answerable across cameras and across time. A log line is not.

Scope discipline Eight object types. Not a knowledge-graph platform. And one hard restriction: no permanent "person" record. A tracked thing is a track with a beginning and an end, not a person with a history. Adding a durable person record would quietly turn this into a face-recognition database.

32
Search the history

Investigators need to look backwards, not only watch live. "Show me everything at Camera 14 between 2 and 4 a.m. last Tuesday."

33
Every human decision is recorded, with a reason

Each acknowledgement, escalation, dismissal, zone edit, sensitivity change and history search is stored as its own object with who, when and why. Incidents don't get quietly edited — their state is derived from these recorded decisions.

Why it fits Accountability stops being a promise on a slide and becomes a side effect of how the database is shaped. Cheap to build. It's much of what separates this from a demo.

34
Different views for different roles

An operator sees live incidents. A supervisor can additionally search history — and every history search is itself recorded as a decision. Most competing teams build a single all-access dashboard.

35
Follow one person across two cameras

When someone walks out of Camera A's view and into Camera B's, the system recognises them as the same figure using their overall appearance, and keeps the same identity. It answers the operator's actual next question: which way did they go?

Why it fits A genuine capability upgrade, and the working embodiment of the brief's "existing CCTV, not siloed" framing. Also the most visually impressive moment available.

Risk Accuracy collapses at night, in fog and on low-resolution feeds — exactly the P2 conditions that matter most. Build it, demonstrate it in good light, say plainly that it degrades.

Hard restriction Matching happens only against figures currently being tracked, in a short time window — never against a stored library of past individuals. A permanent library is face recognition wearing a different hat.

36
Check plates against a watchlist

Turns "we saw a vehicle" into "we saw a flagged vehicle," which is the actual operational value.

Two honest caveats There's no real registry to connect to, so this runs against a clearly labelled mock list. And it's only ever as accurate as the plate reading beneath it — strong at a controlled crossing point, weak on an open road at night.

Usable by any force

the thesis
37
Each organisation's data stays its own

Every record belongs to an organisation, and permissions are enforced at that boundary. Several forces can use the same platform without seeing each other's data.

38
Zones that mean nothing in particular — deliberately

A zone is a shape plus a label describing what kind of place it is: fence linegatewaterlineperimeterpassrestricted area

So a border fence line and a naval jetty perimeter are the same primitive with a different label. No force-specific code anywhere.

Why it fits The brief says "strategic installations," not just "border."

Risk Generality is a discipline problem. The temptation will be to special-case one force to make a demo work. Give in once and the whole thesis collapses.

39
Everything specific lives in one configuration file

Zones, which things to look for, rule thresholds, how long to keep data, which severity means what. Adapting to a new force or a new site means writing a file — and the change itself is recorded as a decision.

40
Posts sync to headquarters, and stay in charge of their own data

Each site is the authority on its own records; headquarters aggregates. If they ever disagree, the originating site wins — so headquarters can never corrupt what a post recorded.

Note Designed this way because it must survive being disconnected, not because distributed systems are elegant.

Privacy posture

built anyway

Worth knowing the context: roughly 21,500 acres of private farmland lies beyond the Punjab border fence. Farmers cross daily through gates on fixed schedules. The overwhelming majority of people these cameras see are villagers and farmers whose cooperation the force depends on. Face recognition applied to them is a legal, ethical and operational liability.

41
Faces detected, then blurred by default

Faces are found — the brief asks for that — and then de-identified before anything is stored. No link to any identity database. Full recognition exists as a separately authorised module that ships switched off.

The pre-empt The brief's capability list says "face detection" but its outcomes section says "support facial recognition." The answer to that gap: the platform is architecturally capable of recognition and deliberately ships it disabled, because a border recognition system with no lawful matching database and no privacy assessment is a liability, not a capability. Stronger than either building it or ignoring the line.

42
Data expires on a schedule

A defined retention window per organisation, with automatic deletion. Not a policy document — a running job.

High-level design

How it's actually built

The rest of this document is diagrams. Each plate has a caption explaining what its parts do — enough to understand the shape of the system without reading code.

The five rules everything follows

Rule 1
No cloud, anywhere important

Local database, local files, local login, local AI models. A disconnected post runs everything.

Rule 2
A post is a whole system, not a sensor

Every post analyses, decides, stores, alerts and serves its own screen. Headquarters only adds; removing it breaks nothing locally.

Rule 3
The data model is the only doorway

Nothing bypasses it. Camera detections, outside sensors, operator decisions and syncing all become the same kinds of object.

Rule 4
Force-specific means configuration

No "if this is the Navy" anywhere in the code. Zones, rules and thresholds are loaded from a file.

Rule 5
Operator attention is the budget

Output target is roughly eight incidents a shift. Anything that adds to what a human must look at has to justify itself.

Rule 6
A graphics card is a bonus, never a requirement

Runs on an ordinary processor. No feature may depend on special hardware to exist at all.

Read the arrow labels, not just the boxes — the data changes shape five times, and that is the actual design. Video becomes chosen frames (most are skipped, which is what makes this run on ordinary hardware), frames become findings, findings become events, events become incidents, incidents become alerts. Note where the funnel narrows: millions of findings a day, and roughly eight incidents a shift reaching a human.

Two things in this picture matter more than they look. First, findings are thrown away — an event is the earliest thing written to disk, which is why the database stays small enough for a remote post. Second, outside sensors enter at L3, not at the front: because information only ever moves upward and no layer may skip another, a radar alert can be dropped straight into the shared data model without any part of the camera pipeline knowing it exists. That single property is what makes the drone answer possible at all.

Why the boxes are coloured in three groups: those are three separate running programs. Analysis is kept apart from the service on purpose — it is the part that consumes the processor, and it must never be slowed down by someone opening a dashboard page.

Information only moves upward, and no layer may skip another. That constraint is what lets the hook layer inject an outside sensor's alert straight into the data model without touching any of the camera-processing layers beneath it. Running alongside all six: the configuration loader, permissions, the blindness ladder, and organisation separation.

One subtle but important consequence. Wait-and-confirm thresholds are written in seconds, not in numbers of frames. If they were counted in frames, the same setting would silently mean four times longer on a slower machine — and each force would need its own tuning, breaking Rule 4. Two planning consequences follow: cross-camera matching becomes a bonus rather than the headline demo, and the configuration switch becomes the primary showcase, since it needs no computation at all.

Three of these fields carry most of the weight. An event records where it came from — which is why an anti-drone sensor's alert lands in the same table, feeds the same grouping logic and appears on the same screen as a camera detection, with no parallel plumbing. An event also records whether its source was simulated, set once per adapter so it can't be forgotten. And a tracked thing can point at an earlier track it turned out to be the same as — which is how cross-camera following works without ever building a library of people.
A quiet but important choice: what is not stored

Analysing eight cameras produces millions of raw observations a day with almost no lasting value. Those stay in memory and are thrown away. What gets saved is an event plus a small evidence bundle — a thumbnail, the path walked, the zone, the rule name, the confidence. Video clips are cut from the rolling buffer only when someone asks for one.

The result: the database stays small enough to live on modest hardware at a remote post — which Rule 1 requires.

Deliberately absent
No Redis
Live updates ride Postgres' own NOTIFY / LISTEN. The database is already there and already reliable; a second store to run and monitor buys nothing.
No Kafka or queue service
Retry queues are ordinary database tables. Durable, inspectable with a query, and one less thing to keep alive at a remote post.
No object storage service
Evidence is files on a mounted disk. An S3-style service is swappable in later, but it is not needed and would be one more moving part.
No cloud anything
No hosted database, no external AI service, no internet login, no managed queue. Not a setting — an architectural commitment.
Six running pieces per node, and that is the entire stack: three programs, one database, one optional message broker, one disk volume. Nothing else has to be installed, licensed, kept patched, or explained to whoever inherits the post.

The one clever substitution. Most systems shaped like this reach for Redis to push live updates to the screen. Here the analysis program writes an event and immediately fires Postgres' built-in NOTIFY; the service program is already sitting on a LISTEN and forwards it to the browser. Same result, zero extra infrastructure in the path that matters. If throughput ever demands a real queue, adding one is a local change — but starting with one would have meant a component to power, monitor and repair at a post that may not have mains electricity.

Why the analysis program is separate from the service program. It is the part that consumes the processor, and it must never be slowed by someone loading a dashboard page. It also means camera capacity and screen capacity can grow independently.

Two databases, not one — the load-bearing detail. Sharing a single database between post and headquarters would make the whole distributed design a fiction: syncing would silently become an internal query, and the disconnection test would prove nothing. Each side owns its own storage, and the only thing that ever crosses between them is messages.

What this design deliberately leaves out

Listed so the gaps read as decisions rather than oversights:

  • No dispatch or patrol routing. Getting the right team to the right place is the real operational gap, but it's outside our five chosen problems. The post-local architecture makes it a clean addition later.
  • No fixing broken cameras. The system notices a dead or frozen feed, because the blindness ladder needs that. Doing anything about it is out of scope.
  • No seeing drones. The connection layer is the answer instead.
  • No cloud, no managed services. Rule 1 is a commitment, not a setting.
  • No force-specific code paths. Rule 4.

Status

Settled, and still open

Settled

Hardwareundecided → design to the floor

No graphics card committed, so the design targets an ordinary processor and treats a graphics card as a bonus. Two knock-on effects worth knowing: cross-camera following drops from headline to bonus, and face detection becomes incident-triggered rather than continuous on a slower machine.

If a graphics card does turn up, it's one line of configuration and nothing else changes.

Cameras at oncefour

Enough to demonstrate following someone between cameras and to run a two-configuration deployment, without needing heavy optimisation work.

Layoutone computer, two nodes

Two genuinely separate databases with one shared connection between them — which is what makes cutting that connection a real test rather than a mock-up.

Databaseone schema, two writers

A single schema file governs structure; the web side reads through it and the analysis side writes directly. Drift is the risk, handled by generating definitions and checking them at startup.

Still open

Second scenario footagehighest remaining risk

Because the configuration switch is now the primary showcase, the stakes on this went up. It needs a fence-line scenario and a waterline or perimeter scenario to be something you can watch rather than something you claim.

The fallback — running the second configuration against a still scene — still shows the switch, but the "same code, different force" point lands considerably softer.

Power budget at the post

Some posts have no mains electricity at all, so "runs at the post" needs a real number attached. Designing for an ordinary processor makes this easier to answer honestly than a graphics-card machine would.

Timeline and who builds what

The four-way split maps cleanly onto the six layers, but one person's share — judgement, data model and connections together — is visibly heavier than the others. If time gets tight, post-to-HQ syncing is the first thing to drop, since the disconnection demonstration works on the retry queue alone.

Two things that must stay visibly labelled

The anti-drone sensor feed and the vehicle watchlist both run against invented data. Both are flagged as simulated in the data itself — not by remembering to mention it — and both must be stated plainly when shown. A made-up sensor feed or registry presented as real is the single thing that would sink an otherwise strong project.

Plain-language glossary

BOP
Border Out Post — a small manned post along the international border.
Check post
A controlled crossing point on a road, where traffic stops and is inspected.
CCTV
Ordinary security cameras. In this context, network-connected ones already installed.
RTSP / ONVIF
The standard, universal languages IP cameras use to send video and be controlled. Speaking these is what makes "no special hardware" possible.
ANPR
Automatic Number Plate Recognition — reading a vehicle's registration plate from video.
Detection
Finding a thing in a single frame and drawing a box round it.
Tracking
Keeping the same identity on that thing across many frames, so it's understood as one moving object rather than a new object each frame.
Virtual fence
A line or shape drawn on the camera's view. Crossing it is an event. No physical sensor involved.
False positive
An alarm for something that didn't matter — a cow, wind in the grass, rain. The main thing this project is built to reduce.
Edge
Doing the computing at the post itself rather than sending video somewhere central. Necessary when there's no reliable connection.
Cloud-independent
Nothing depends on an internet service. Everything — analysis, storage, screen, logins — runs on local equipment.
Data model
The fixed set of object types the system knows about, and how they connect. Here it's the only way anything enters the system.
Hook
A defined connection point for sending data in or out. This project uses one shared format for all of them.
Store-and-forward
When the link is down, keep records locally and send them when it returns.
Cross-camera matching
Recognising that the figure now on Camera B is the one who just left Camera A, using overall appearance rather than face.
Configuration profile
A file describing one site's zones, what to watch for, and how sensitive to be. Adapting to a new force means writing one of these.
Incident
Many related detections grouped into one thing a human deals with. The unit of work on the operator's screen.
Action
A recorded human decision — who did what, when, and why. Stored as a first-class object, not a side note.
